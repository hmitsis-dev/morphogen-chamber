what this is

this is being drawn, live, by real ICMP echo packets — ordinary-looking pings — flowing between two servers right now. nothing here is fake or pre-rendered.

how

one service sends a normal-looking ping roughly a dozen times a second. hidden in the data section, after the ping header, is a small binary frame: two magic bytes, a sequence number, a timestamp, and four numbers. the other service sniffs every echo request that reaches it, checks for the magic bytes, decodes the frame, and pushes it to this page over a websocket.

  • huewhich color the particles and grid drift toward
  • energyparticle brightness, size, and spawn rate
  • speedhow fast the flow field and grid move
  • bursttriggers the expanding ring you'll see occasionally

click anywhere

clicking or tapping the page does two things. right away, a faint ring appears at your cursor — that part is fake, purely drawn client-side so it feels responsive. at the same time, this page asks the receiver to ping the sender for real, requesting an extra burst. a moment later a brighter ring blooms from the center — that one is real, having actually crossed the network and come back. the gap between the two rings is the round-trip time, made visible.

is any of this secure?

there's nothing sensitive to protect here — the payload is just the four numbers above, nothing personal or secret. but to be honest about the mechanism: ICMP has no built-in authentication. the receiver only accepts packets whose source IP matches the sender it's configured for, but source IPs can be spoofed on networks that don't filter outbound traffic, so in principle someone could forge packets and inject their own values into this page. it doesn't matter here — there's nothing to steal and nothing that actually breaks — but it's a disguise, not a security boundary. the one real piece of user input, the click-to-nudge endpoint, is rate limited per visitor and overall so it can't be turned into a flood.
